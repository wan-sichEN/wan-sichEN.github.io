Brake Caliper

Designed and optimized a lightweight bicycle brake caliper to meet ISO braking standards.

Year: 2026 Status: Complete

Overview

For ME240: Intro to Mechanical Design and Manufacturing, my team and I were tasked with designing brake calipers made from SLS Nylon 12 for the rear wheel of a bicycle.

The goal was to meet the ISO 4210-2 brake distance standard while minimizing weight. I served as Project Management Lead, but also took ownership of CAD, FEA and topology optimization, and technical documentation efforts for the left caliper.

Defining Requirements

First, my team created a Needs Table and Metrics Table (Fig. 1, 2, 3) based on market research of caliper brakes and ISO 4210-2 and 4210-4 requirements.

Additionally, my team defined a design space (Fig. 4) constrained by the bike wheel, frame, and mounting points. This identified the physical boundaries of our calipers that we later used for CAD and simulation.

Design Process

I led hand calculations to determine the loads experienced by both caliper brakes for simulation. These were a maximum tension force of 360 N and friction force of 202.36 N. Using these values, my team ran topology optimization (Fig. 5, 6) on each caliper’s design space to identify areas where material should be retained to maximize stiffness and where it could be removed to reduce weight and cost. The results reaffirmed our approach of removing material from the caliper interiors, which contribute less to structural stiffness, while adding material to the outer edges.

Using these design choices, our initial test design achieved maximum stresses of ~30 MPa in relevant regions (Fig. 7, 8), below the 50 MPa yield strength of Nylon 12 but above our calculated 27.41 MPa fatigue strength. Total deflection was 6.222 mm (Fig. 9), below the 11 mm allowable limit. Regional stresses exceeding 50 MPa near the pivot points were attributed to unphysical constraint behavior and excluded from evaluation.

Testing & Iteration

Our initial test design was 3D-printed and tested on a real bicycle according to ISO 4210-2. It was able to stop the bicycle in 9.9 m, well below the 15 m ISO limit. We also measured the actual user input force to be around 150 N instead of 180 N, and used this value in subsequent simulations. This large braking margin allowed us to pursue further weight reduction while maintaining ISO compliance.

To reduce weight, I iteratively removed material from low-stress regions identified through FEA and refined areas where material removal caused excessive stress or deflection. After many iterations, we minimized the relevant stresses of the left and right caliper to 28.44 MPa and 35.37 MPa, respectively, while preserving our material-reduction changes (Fig. 10, 11). See Fig. 12 for the caliper deflections, which are still within the allowable limit.

Our final design (Fig. 13, 14) achieved:

  • 12.2 m braking distance — a 23% increase while remaining ISO compliant
  • 59.2 g total weight — 13.2% lighter than our initial design and 42.2 g lighter than the lightest industry benchmark identified
  • $5.92 manufacturing cost — $0.93 lower than our initial design and $2.08 below the cheapest industry benchmark identified

Future iterations would focus on further reducing localized stresses to below the fatigue strength, so that the brakes can withstand the projected number of cycles.
